Change Order

A marketer changes one field on a live campaign. Behind that field sit dozens of dependent artifacts that now have to change too. This is the flow I designed for that moment: the agent works out what the edit touches, proposes a plan, and moves through it one approved step at a time.

in brief

The problem: editing a live campaign is never just the edit. One change invalidates dozens of downstream artifacts.

What I designed: the flow where the agent infers the cascade, proposes an ordered plan, and executes it one approved gate at a time, with a from-to summary and a version to roll back to.

The payoff: a two-field edit safely becomes sixty-four rebuilt experiences, and the person is never surprised.

01 where this comes from

Create was linear. Change is consequential.

I designed the create flow first. It was a sequence: prompt, then build, one stage at a time, forward. Change order is the harder half of the same product, and it does not move in a straight line. When a person edits a live campaign, the real problem is not the edit. It is everything the edit sets off.

A campaign here is not one object. It is a tree: experiments, treatments, segments, offers, and dozens of downstream experiences generated from the campaign's settings. Change the end date and the priority, and those experiences are no longer valid. Something has to notice that, plan the rebuild, and keep the person in control of work the system is redoing on their behalf. That is the design problem.

02 the flow

One conversation, from finding a campaign to changing it safely

The whole thing happens in one thread. The conversation lives on the right, the live campaign on the left. The person never leaves to go do the work. They ask, they review, they approve.

  1. ask

    Find it in plain language

    "Find all Payroll targeted-offer campaigns created in the last 3 months." The agent spins up a project and shows its work: reading the brief, searching, compiling. No query builder, no filters to learn.

  2. results

    Get a real, workable table

    Twenty-two campaigns come back as a sortable, paginated table, not a wall of chat text. The person opens one and sees its full structure: campaign meta, and the nested tree of experiments, treatments, segments, and offers.

  3. request

    Ask for the change

    "Change the end date to 31 August and priority to high." Two fields. The person is not thinking about the sixty-four downstream experiences those two fields invalidate. The system has to.

  4. plan

    See the plan before anything moves

    The agent proposes an ordered plan: change the fields, review a summary, update the campaign. It asks "does this look ok?" before it touches anything. The person can redirect before the agent commits.

  5. execute

    Watch it happen, one gate at a time

    Task one runs. The changed fields light up on the live artifact. Then the agent pauses for approval before task two. Nothing cascades without a yes.

  6. cascade

    Two fields become sixty-four rebuilt experiences

    On approval, the agent regenerates every dependent experience and bumps the campaign from v1 to v2. The system understood what the two changes meant, and rebuilt everything downstream, with a version to fall back to.

03 the core move

Show the plan, then show the difference

Two moments carry the trust. First, the agent commits to a plan out loud, in order, before it acts. Second, it shows the change as a plain before-and-after, so the person is never guessing what actually moved.

04 decisions

What I chose, and what it cost

  1. trade 01

    Plan first, act second

    I gave up the speed of an instant edit to buy orientation. Before anything changes, the person sees the ordered plan and can stop it. For a change that cascades, being able to say no in advance matters more than saving a click.

  2. trade 02

    Gate every step, not just the start

    One approval up front would have been cleaner. But the cascade is where trust is won or lost, so I put a checkpoint at each task. The cost is more taps. The gain is that the person is never surprised.

  3. trade 03

    Show the diff, not the whole object

    The campaign has hundreds of fields. I chose a from-to summary that names exactly what moved, and highlighted those same fields on the live artifact so the two views agree.

  4. trade 04

    Make the cascade visible, and reversible

    Marking each regenerated experience Edited and bumping the version to v2 turns an invisible side effect into something the person can see and roll back. Consequence, made legible.

  5. trade 05

    Keep results as artifacts, not chat

    Search results are a real table with sort and pagination. The conversation drives the work, but the work itself stays a first-class object you can act on.

05 honest limits
not solved yet

Partial cascades. Today the cascade is all-or-nothing on approval. Accepting the metadata change but holding the experience regeneration is not designed yet.

Conflicting concurrent edits. The version bump gives a fallback, but two people changing the same campaign at once is not handled.

Explaining why a change cascades. The person sees that sixty-four experiences changed. They cannot yet ask why those specific ones. Making the dependency graph legible is the next problem worth solving.

06 reflection

The through-line in my work is caring about where things go after they are made, not just the screen where they are made. Change order is that idea in its purest form. The hard part was never the edit. It was designing the seam between a person's simple intent and a system's complicated consequences, so the person stays in control of a change they could not fully see, and trusts the system precisely because it showed them its plan first.

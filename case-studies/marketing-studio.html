The problem

One campaign. Ten tools. Zero flow.

Picture a marketer at Intuit about to launch a personalized campaign. They start with a brief. Then they wait on content SLAs. Chase targeting teams. Coordinate experimentation. Go back to content for revisions. Then approvals. Then activation. Across 10+ tools and 5+ teams.

Building the actual in-product displays, upsells, and cross-sells means more back and forth in Figma, waiting on designers, and another round of handoffs. That is one campaign. It takes 1 to 2 weeks. And a marketer might run dozens a month.

Diagram, swap in laterHow one campaign for one product gets built today, the full fragmented workflow

That is one campaign, for one product. Multiply it across every product Intuit markets. That is the problem.

The vision

Tell me what you need. I'll handle the rest.

Marketing Studio was built on one idea: what if every marketer had a single place to go, and AI did the coordination and grunt work across every step of the campaign workflow?

From a business view, it replaces a fragmented ecosystem of tools and teams with a single agentic AI layer. Redundant tools get eliminated. Team dependencies shrink. Marketers stop coordinating and start creating. The homepage became the entry point: natural language intent in, agent-built next moves out.

The challenge

Designing for something that did not exist yet

No UX playbook. No precedent inside Intuit. No obvious reference points outside it. The challenge was not visual. It was conceptual. Three questions shaped everything.

  • 01
    How do you show AI progress without losing trust? Marketers are accountable for every campaign. They needed visibility into what the AI was doing and why, without being overwhelmed.
  • 02
    How do you structure a workspace for non-deterministic workflows? Agentic workflows are dynamic. The AI decides the path. The design had to accommodate that while still feeling coherent and learnable.
  • 03
    How do you decide what belongs in the product? With AI, almost anything feels buildable. Feature decisions had to be grounded in user need, not engineering possibility.

Early research

We looked wide before we looked deep

We studied how other AI tools solved the same problems: conversational (ChatGPT, Gemini), agentic (Cursor, Replit), workspace (Confluence), creative (Napkin AI). Three patterns shaped our direction.

  • →
    Output centricity. The artifact, not the conversation, is the primary focus.
  • →
    Progressive transparency. Show enough for trust, not everything under the hood.
  • →
    Guided autonomy. Clear decision points, no micromanagement.
The IA framework

One mental model. Every workflow.

Before any screen, a more basic question: how is this product even structured? The answer had to hold for every workflow, every user type, and capabilities we had not built yet. I defined five organizing pillars.

Scenario mapping laid out the full range of user tasks the platform needed to support. A set of How Might We questions then grounded every design decision that followed.

  • HMW
    give marketers visibility into what the AI is doing and why?
  • HMW
    structure workflows that are dynamic without feeling unpredictable?
  • HMW
    centralize tools to reduce campaign delivery fragmentation?
  • HMW
    support multiple workflow types without multiplying complexity?
The pattern language

A pattern for every interaction

With no existing patterns to follow, we built our own. Every interaction maps to a pattern; every pattern serves one or more of the five pillars. It became the UX vocabulary for agentic AI at Intuit.

Research and validation

Designing with evidence, not assumption

Research happened across three focused efforts, each tied to a specific design decision.

Usability testing

Every issue got mapped to JJ Garrett's five layers, so we knew not just what broke but how deep. We focused where the gap between expected and actual difficulty was widest, measured with SUS and NPS.

Chart, swap in laterExpected task difficulty vs experience, with SUS and NPS
Chart, swap in laterKano model, feature prioritization for the agentic MVP

Three findings shaped the design directly.

  • 01
    Trust but verify was the dominant mindset. Marketers were optimistic about AI but needed full transparency at every approval stage. This validated the reasoning card in the process pane.
  • 02
    Terminology confusion created friction. Users did not share the system's mental model. This drove a terminology audit across the platform.
  • 03
    Verification gaps were the biggest barrier to adoption. This became a priority for the action bar design and the change-differentiation model.

Research with NotebookLM

Every transcript and analysis went into NotebookLM. PMs, engineering, and design query the research directly instead of waiting on a designer to dig.

Kano model testing

Kano separated the MVP's must-haves from delighters, with user evidence deciding, not stakeholder opinion. Must-haves shipped. Everything else went to backlog with a reason attached.

Takeaways

What building a product that did not exist taught me

  • 01
    Scale is a decision you make early. The pattern language and IA were not polish. They were the foundation everything else was built on. Without them, every new flow would have started from zero.
  • 02
    Agentic UX is about trust, not intelligence. Marketers did not need the AI to be smarter. They needed it to be legible. Clarity, transparency, and control mattered more than capability.
  • 03
    Patterns over screens. Non-deterministic workflows do not scale screen by screen. A pattern language gives every new workflow a foundation to build on.
  • 04
    Ambiguity is the real brief. We were designing while the architecture was still being figured out. Deciding, documenting, and keeping the team aligned through constant change mattered as much as the design work itself.

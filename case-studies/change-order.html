Change Order

A marketer changes one field on a live campaign. Behind that field sit dozens of dependent artifacts that now have to change too. This is the flow I designed for that moment: the agent works out what the edit touches, proposes a plan, and moves through it one approved step at a time.

In brief
  • The problem: editing a live campaign is never just the edit. One change invalidates dozens of downstream artifacts.
  • What I designed: the flow where the agent infers the cascade, proposes an ordered plan, and executes it one approved gate at a time, with a from-to summary and a version to roll back to.
  • The payoff: a two-field edit safely becomes sixty-four rebuilt experiences, and the marketer is never surprised.

Where this comes from

Create was linear. Change is consequential.

I designed the create flow first. It was a sequence: prompt, then build, one stage at a time, forward. Change order is the harder half of the same product, and it does not move in a straight line. When a marketer edits a live campaign, the real problem is not the edit. It is everything the edit sets off.

A campaign here is not one object. It is a tree: experiments, treatments, segments, offers, and dozens of downstream experiences generated from the campaign's settings. Change the end date and the priority, and those experiences are no longer valid.

Something has to notice that, plan the rebuild, and keep the marketer in control of work the system is redoing on their behalf. That is the design problem.

The flow

One conversation, from finding a campaign to changing it safely

The whole thing happens in one thread. The conversation lives on the right, the live campaign on the left. The marketer never leaves to go do the work. They ask, they review, they approve.

  1. Step 01
    Ask

    Find it in plain language.

    "Find all Payroll targeted-offer campaigns created in the last 3 months." The agent spins up a project and shows its work as it goes: reading the brief, searching, compiling. No query builder, no filters to learn.

  2. Step 02
    Results

    Get a real, workable table.

    Twenty-two campaigns come back as a sortable, paginated table, not a wall of chat text. The marketer opens one and sees its full structure: campaign meta, and the nested tree of experiments, treatments, segments, and offers.

  3. Step 03
    Request

    Ask for the change.

    "Change the end date to 31 August and priority to high." Two fields. The marketer is not thinking about the sixty-four downstream experiences those two fields invalidate. The system has to.

  4. Step 04
    Plan

    See the plan before anything moves.

    The agent reads the request and proposes an ordered plan: change the fields, review a summary, update the campaign. It asks "does this look ok?" before it touches anything. The marketer can redirect before the agent commits.

  5. Step 05
    Execute

    Watch it happen, one gate at a time.

    Task one runs. The changed fields light up on the live artifact so the marketer can see exactly what moved. Then the agent pauses: "does this look ok? I'll proceed to task two." Nothing cascades without a yes.

  6. Step 06
    Cascade

    The two-field edit becomes sixty-four rebuilt experiences.

    On approval, the agent regenerates every dependent experience and bumps the campaign from v1 to v2. The marketer made two changes. The system understood what those two changes meant, and rebuilt everything downstream, with a version to fall back to.

The core move

Show the plan, then show the difference

Two moments carry the trust in this flow. First, the agent commits to a plan out loud, in order, before it acts. Second, it shows the change as a plain before-and-after, so the marketer is never guessing what actually moved.

Decisions

What I chose, and what it cost

Every decision here was a tradeoff. These are the ones that shaped the flow.

  • 01
    Plan first, act second. I gave up the speed of an instant edit to buy orientation. Before anything changes, the marketer sees the ordered plan and can stop it. For a change that cascades, being able to say no in advance matters more than saving a click.
  • 02
    Gate every step, not just the start. One approval up front would have been cleaner. But the cascade is where trust is won or lost, so I put a checkpoint at each task. The cost is more taps. The gain is that the marketer is never surprised by what the system did on their behalf.
  • 03
    Show the diff, not the whole object. The campaign has hundreds of fields. Re-rendering all of them at review time would bury the two that changed. I chose a From to To summary that names exactly what moved, and highlighted those same fields on the live artifact so the two views agree.
  • 04
    Make the cascade visible, and reversible. When sixty-four experiences regenerate at once, it can feel like the system running away with the work. Marking each one Edited and bumping the version to v2 turns a silent side effect into something the marketer can see and roll back.
  • 05
    Keep results as artifacts, not chat. Search results are a real table with sort and pagination, not text in the thread. The conversation drives the work, but the work itself stays a first-class object you can act on.
Honest limits

What this flow does not solve yet

  • Partial cascades. Today the cascade is all-or-nothing on approval. A marketer might want to accept the metadata change but hold the experience regeneration. That branch is not designed yet.
  • Conflicting concurrent edits. The version bump gives a fallback, but two people changing the same campaign at once is not handled. The flow assumes a single editor.
  • Explaining why a change cascades. The marketer sees that sixty-four experiences changed. They cannot yet ask the agent why those specific ones, and not others. Making the dependency graph legible is the next problem worth solving.
What I took from it

Reflection

My work keeps returning to where things go after they are made. Change order is that idea as one flow. The hard part was never the edit. It was the gap between what a marketer means by two fields and everything the system must do to honor them.

The answer was the plan: say it before you do it.
